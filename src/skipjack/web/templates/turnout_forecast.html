Three scenarios

The model does not pick one turnout level. It calibrates each party's turnout to the two most recent midterms, 2022 (low) and 2018 (high), and sets a central scenario between them using a rule written before the 2026 numbers came in. The spread between low and high is the real uncertainty: the 90% intervals within each scenario are narrow because they carry only county-level noise.

How the central scenario is set

Each party starts halfway between its 2022 and 2018 turnout. Four measures of 2026 engagement, each compared with 2022, can move it 10 points of the way toward one year or the other, between 20% and 80% of the way to 2018.

How the model works

The model, fit in R, is a regression of ballots cast over active registration by county and party in six elections: the 2018 and 2022 midterms, the 2022 and 2026 primaries and the 2024 primary and general. It separates each election's statewide party level from a lasting pattern by county and party, with county census measures (education, age, mobility, home ownership). Each 2026 county-and-party group is then adjusted for how its registrants differ from 2022's in age and in how recently they registered, measured from the statewide voter list. The scenarios supply the statewide level.

Leaving one midterm out at a time and giving the model that year's true statewide party levels, its county turnout rates missed by these many percentage points on average. The naive forecast uses the other midterm's county pattern instead:
